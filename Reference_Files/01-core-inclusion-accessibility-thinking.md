## Accessibility as Emotion + Function

Accessibility is a broad subject that positively impacts everyone. It is full of opportunity and is as much about emotion as it is about function. Compliance alone will not give an organisation or its users what is needed.

## Language of Accessibility & Avoiding Otherness

Finding the language of accessibility within an organisation is tricky. “Accessibility” is a loaded word that can contextualise problems for user groups but can also foster a sense of otherness. Accessibility must be discussed as part of the mainstream, not an add‑on.

## Inclusion Language in Organisational Strategy

20% of the population has a disability. Words like “Everyone”, “Diversity”, and “Inclusion” in organisational policies imply that accessibility is essential to achieving strategic objectives. None of these terms are caveated with “except disabled people”.

## Games, Media & Social Currency

Games and media are social currency. If someone is excluded from participating due to an impairment, they are excluded from both the experience and the social conversation. Inclusive content design prevents this exclusion.

## Shift‑Left Accessibility & Worthwhile Investment

Organisations must shift from “making things accessible” to “building accessible things”. Shift‑left accessibility is efficient, reduces rework, and improves reach, usability, engagement, loyalty, and brand perception.

## Research Participation & Missing Disabled Voices

If disabled people cannot participate in research due to methodology or platform, the resulting data is skewed. With disabled people representing at least 20% of any audience, excluding them removes critical insight.

## Demographics vs Human Characteristics

Medical-condition demographics are not useful for design research. They do not reveal lived experience, coping strategies, or capabilities. Insight comes from understanding behaviours, contexts, and barriers.

## Anecdotes vs Data

Anecdotes are useful for understanding barriers and forming hypotheses, but they are not data. Qualitative studies rarely reach statistical significance. Anecdotes can become “conventional wisdom” if misused.

## Accessibility Culture Over Compliance

Accessibility is fundamentally about organisational culture, which comes from leadership. Accessibility managers must nurture belief and practice, often through mechanisms like Accessibility Champion Networks.

## Emotional, Functional & Technical Accessibility

Accessibility has three components: emotional, functional, and technical. All three must be balanced. Claims that accessibility undermines design should be challenged; only bad design makes design look bad.

## Accessibility as Transient, Environmental, Situational

Accessibility is mainstream because impairments can be temporary or situational.

Examples include:

- Battery preservation and colour contrast
- One‑handed use while carrying items
- Mainstream captioning usage
- Night‑time cognitive/motor impairment

## Designing for Real‑World Contexts

Dieter Rams: “The one and only cardinal sin in design is not designing for the reality in which people live.” Research must reflect real contexts, demographics, and environmental barriers.

## Proactive Inaccessibility & Exclusion Lists

Teams should ask early: “Who are we willing to exclude?” This clarifies scope and prevents unintentional exclusion later. Exclusion may be appropriate for certain groups (e.g., under‑5s for a news app) but must not expand during development.

## Social Model of Disability in UX

The Social Model states that people are disabled by societal barriers, not impairments. Every design decision includes or excludes users. Inaccessibility is proactive; inclusive practice must be embedded early.

## Alt‑Text Ownership & Editorial Intent

Alt text must convey editorial intent.

Responsibility:

- Marketing → brand imagery
- UX → icons and functional imagery
- Content → editorial images

Infographics should be summarised; decorative images can be silent; logos should be named.

## Bi‑Media Production & Audio Description

Bi‑media production scripts content as if there are no pictures, making it inherently accessible. Audio description is then added only where needed. Many content types naturally suit this approach.

## Caption UX & Safe Zones

Caption UX requires:

- Avoiding covering mouths (for lip readers)
- Preserving access to on‑screen graphics
- Supporting caption‑first users

Shot composition and graphic layout must account for caption safe zones.

## Affordances & Conventions in Cognitive Accessibility

Affordances indicate how elements behave. Conventions are expected norms. Both shape cognitive schema. Ignoring them increases cognitive load and creates barriers, especially for users with cognitive impairments.

## Font Accessibility Fundamentals

Choosing an accessible font is foundational. Applying accessibility guidance to an unsuitable font is “accessibility lipstick on a typographic pig”. Designers must understand reading behaviour and how to balance brand, platform, and user needs.

## HTML as Screen Reader UX

HTML structure is UX for screen reader users. Designers must understand code behaviour, semantics, tab order, and interactive behaviour. Technical choices can be discriminatory if not considered ethically.

## Compliance vs Accessibility

Compliance answers only one question: “Is it compliant?” It does not guarantee accessibility or usability for disabled users. Compliance satisfies legal and policy requirements but is not the full solution.

## WCAG as Foundation, Not Ambition

WCAG levels (A/AA/AAA) are a baseline. As organisations mature, guidelines should be treated as minimums, not goals. WCAG is comprehensive but necessarily complex and organisationally agnostic.

## Auditing: Purpose & Limits

Audits answer:

- Where are we now?
- How far have we come?
- How consistent are we?

Audits are waterfall tools, not suited to agile development. Use rolling user data + automated testing for ongoing accessibility work.

## Accessible Components & Design Systems

Accessible design systems reduce workload. Components should meet WCAG and EAA Annex 1 requirements. Documentation should include design intent, handover briefs, developer techniques, reference implementations, research, and test scripts.

## Machines Benefit from Accessibility

Standards-based development improves:

- Browser/AT compatibility
- Internationalisation
- Performance and data cost
- Metadata quality
- Search indexing
- AI Training

Progressive enhancement supports global audiences and reduces breakage risk.

## Compliance vs Equivalence in Employment

Vendors must evidence compliance; employers must evidence equivalence. A system may be compliant but still take disabled users far longer to complete tasks, reducing productivity and effectively excluding them.

## Accessibility Policies vs Statements

Policies reflect what an organisation already does and can maintain. Statements express intent and improvement goals. Policies set expectations for teams and users; statements contextualise the journey.

# LLM‑Training Format: Instruction / Input / Output

Each triple is self‑contained, domain‑specific, and designed to teach the model how to act, not just what to know.

## Accessibility as Emotion + Function

### Instruction

Explain to a product team why accessibility must be treated as both an emotional and functional requirement, not just a compliance task.

### Input

A team believes accessibility is only about meeting WCAG and wants to deprioritise emotional considerations.

### Output

Accessibility must be framed as both emotional and functional because users experience products holistically. Emotional accessibility ensures people feel included, respected, and able to participate without stigma. Functional accessibility ensures the product works for diverse needs. Compliance alone does not guarantee either outcome. Teams should integrate emotional and functional considerations into design reviews, user research, and acceptance criteria, ensuring each release includes at least one measurable improvement to both areas.

# A De­signed Ex­pe­ri­ence for Every­one

## A principle for ensuring a consistent and considered design for everyone.
“Provide a designed experience for everyone.” Sounds simple. Surely this is what we already do. But do we?

We developed this principle as a way to ask better questions about our design work, which in turn improves the assumptions we make. Ultimately, better assumptions mean better decisions.

But let’s pull this principle apart, starting with the concept of a “designed experience.”

A designed experience means applying design intent to how something should be perceived or how it should work and operate. Delivering a designed experience implements that intent, meaning we’ve considered and created an intentional experience for our audience.

Crucially, design in this context covers the whole experience. Not just the visual aspects but the unseen bits too, such as the information flow, the language used, and any interactions we need to guide our users through.

The second part of this principle mentions “everyone.” It’s important to define what we mean by everyone. Not all users will experience a website or app visually. A screen reader or other assistive technology user, such as a Braille reader, may primarily hear or feel the experience. We need to apply design intent to those experiences too. We shouldn’t build only for visual experience and leave the other experiences to chance.

By defining what we consider a designed experience to be upfront, we set the standard to which we will hold ourselves. And by defining who we believe is part of “everyone,” we identify who we seek to serve.

Taken together, they form an excellent starting point for creating inclusive products.

# Cognitive Design Principles

Cognitive design as a practice came out of studies in user interaction with consumer products in the 1970s, and by the 80s their use was commonplace in the product design industry. Parts of these principles have transferred into the digital space as “Usability Heuristics” but they have an issue. They are like all the other principal frameworks; they are relevant but only from a rather neurotypical perspective. Why? Because they predate the recent upsurge in interest and appreciation of neurodiversity within user groups.

## Standard Elements

Many user errors are actually caused by design inconsistencies in how things work, so ensure that similar elements work the same way. Standard design patterns help prevent errors which is why design systems, design conventions, and web standards are so important.

Users should not have to re-learn the interactions of common tasks locally when they have already learned them universally.

All functional designs should have audio and visual consistency from both interactive and informational perspectives.

## Affordances and Signifiers

An affordance is a perceived way in which an element will behave when interacted with. Not all affordances are intentionally designed.

Signifiers are icons, labels, colours or sounds that define how and where actions are to be performed. A signifier tells us what needs to be done and clarifies affordances.

Affordances and Signifiers enable a designer to take advantage of perceptions and expectations.

The concept of both affordances and Signifiers are closely related to that of a standard, but much less consciously determined. Whereas a standard is a formal agreement to eliminate inconsistencies, affordances and signifiers can also be based on an informal convention that has evolved through time.

There should always be a strong relationship between the perception of the need to take any action and the action itself.

## Simplicity

There is such a thing as too much information and this can either be an issue of volume, or information provided in too complex a fashion. In general, visual and UX design best practice strives to simplify both presentation and interactions.

Clutter divides attention and increases cognitive load reducing user response time and accuracy.

## Clear Communication

Another common problem is caused by a user not being able to identify or interpret a message.

There are issues in presenting clear images and messages: **noticeable, distinguishable, and interpretable**.

Important messages must be **noticeable**. They should contrast with their surroundings and be given appropriate priority.

Important messages should be **distinguishable** from surrounding information. Unrelated messages should be easy to distinguish from one another, so the degree of their relationship is clear.

All messages should be **interpretable**. An example of one type of issue is avoiding the use of characters that look alike e.g. 1lI, B8, QOCD, and try to break up long strings in phone numbers e.g. (000) 000–0000

Interpretation is key when displaying errors. The technical information is important but non-technical advice should also be included so the user knows what they should do to resolve the issue.

Choose an accessible typeface too… not one that says it is “accessible”. For more information see my previous article

## Redundancies

Sometimes one message or mode of interaction is insufficient. Because mistakes are easy to make and humans have many limitations, it is important to consider the provision of the same information in more than one way and in more than one place. If the user misses or cannot access one cue, they pick up on another.

This can sometimes mean there is duplication of effort (consumption or interaction) on behalf of the user and this needs to be considered by the designer against any risk of error.

Redundancy is not just information but also interaction modes such as point, speak, touch or tab that are cognitively more accessible to different users.

Every system needs the flexibility to support the needs of both novice and expert users, but flexibility should also extend to users with different cognitive requirements such as styles of learning, communication and control.

## Patterns

Humans can be pretty good at spotting and grasping the purpose of patterns. Information presented as a pattern can be understood quickly and accurately whereas if there is no obvious structure or hierarchy people can become confused. In this way grouping information, content, or controls in related outcomes, subjects or functions aids user engagement. Similarly, placing elements in patterns or in a specific context will help indicate to the user what to do.

Patterns enable people to learn, relate and engage as their comprehension is built upon all previous interactions with particular products or services.

## Recognition Rather than Recall

Most users can build up a mental map and understanding of a system to enable them to use it ever more efficiently however some users neither have the short or long-term memory to be able to support this. Make objects, actions, and options obvious and persistent so the user is not dependant on having to remember information from. Help and instruction should be easily identifiable, contextual and easily retrievable, and if user error is persistent a system should be able to detect when a user needs help or advancement.

## Variable Stimuli and the Pop-Out Effect

All humans detect an occasional novel stimulus more readily than a constant one because our senses fatigue easily with continuous exposure.

Terms and Conditions or warnings are notorious for being overlooked since they quickly become part of the background and simply are not noticed. This filtration process is something that is generally seen as a benefit as it is a sign that people are developing strategies from ongoing interactions however in some instances it is important to avoid excessive use of a single way of presenting information, especially when it comes to user commitment or safety.

This is also important to consider when introducing a new proposition or an extension to the provision of an existing service. Unearthing and exposing underused functionality that adds benefit to a user is also important if people bypass something that could be of use to them later.

## Feedback and Notification

As a general rule the sooner and clearer feedback is given, the easier it is to determine if an error has been made or not.

The total absence of feedback prevents a user from knowing if an action has been successful and if a mistake has been made. It also greatly increases the likelihood that user error will be repeated.

As products and platforms can have multiple background applications running, notifications have become a key part of any user experience. These should always be a mixture of visual, audible, and haptic with the user being able to control the individual level of persistence.

Notifications shouldn’t compete with each other for attention but instead should uniquely identify themselves in each communication mode.

## User Control and Choice

Products should always be designed in a way that enables users to choose how they control the interface and what mode they wish to consume content.

From a control perspective, this covers all standard modes of user input: pointing device, keyboard, touch screen, and voice command.

As well as all modes of output: Static Text, Video, Audio, Timed Text or Braille.

All text must be able to be heard; all dialogue should be able to be read and complex interactions such as games should be able to be re-configured.

## Embedding Inclusion into Brand Values and Design Principles

### Instruction  
Explain how inclusive design should be embedded into brand values and design principles.

### Explanation  

Inclusive design must begin at the level of brand values rather than being introduced later as a compliance or delivery requirement. This means that inclusion is treated as a defining characteristic of how the organisation communicates, behaves, and creates experiences.

Design principles should explicitly articulate values such as participation, clarity, flexibility, dignity, and emotional safety. These principles must then be operationalised so they guide decisions about tone, structure, language, interaction, and format.  

To implement this effectively, organisations should:
- Translate values into actionable design principles that can be used in decision-making scenarios  
- Align brand identity systems with inclusive practices so that accessibility reinforces, rather than conflicts with, brand expression  
- Integrate principles into design reviews, ensuring that inclusion is evaluated alongside aesthetic and functional quality  
- Train teams to interpret principles consistently across different projects and contexts  

When inclusion is embedded in this way, it becomes a creative constraint that improves coherence, sharpens focus, and builds trust with audiences who are often excluded.

## Inclusion as a Catalyst for Creativity

### Instruction  
Describe how inclusive design supports creativity rather than limiting it.

### Explanation  

Inclusive design expands creative potential by forcing designers to engage more deeply with meaning, audience diversity, and communication clarity. Constraints such as the need for clarity, flexibility, and multiple modes of engagement require more thoughtful and inventive approaches to storytelling and interaction design.

Practitioners should adopt methodologies such as:
- Constraint-led ideation, where accessibility requirements are used as starting points for innovation  
- Multi-modal storytelling, ensuring ideas can be expressed visually, verbally, and interactively  
- Iterative prototyping with diverse user groups to uncover unexpected insights  

Designing for a wider range of needs leads to more original, resilient, and emotionally resonant outcomes that work across contexts rather than relying on narrow aesthetic conventions.

---

## Inclusive Design Questions as Strategic Tools

### Instruction  
Identify good design questions that drive inclusive content strategies.

### Explanation  

Inclusive design is driven by asking better questions rather than applying static rules. These questions should focus on uncovering assumptions, identifying risks, and revealing opportunities for improvement.

Effective questions include:
- Who might be excluded by this decision, and why?  
- What assumptions are being made about attention, language, memory, or physical ability?  
- How might this experience be perceived under stress, fatigue, or distraction?  
- What would create delight rather than mere accommodation for disabled users?  

These questions should be embedded into design processes such as discovery workshops, design critiques, and usability testing. By consistently applying them, teams improve the quality of their decisions and create more emotionally intelligent and inclusive experiences.

## The Emotional Dimension of Accessibility

### Instruction  
Explain the emotional dimension of accessibility and inclusion in content.

### Explanation  

Accessibility is not solely about usability or compliance; it is fundamentally emotional. Inclusive content reduces anxiety, cognitive load, and feelings of exclusion, while increasing confidence, trust, and a sense of belonging.

To design for emotional accessibility, practitioners should:
- Use clear and respectful language that avoids ambiguity or unnecessary complexity  
- Create predictable structures that reduce uncertainty  
- Anticipate user needs rather than reacting to problems after they occur  
- Design interactions that reinforce confidence and competence  

When emotional accessibility is achieved, users are more likely to engage deeply, return frequently, and advocate for the brand.

## UX Writing and Inclusive Microcopy

### Instruction  
Provide guidance for UX Writers on embedding inclusive design.

### Explanation  

UX Writers play a critical role in inclusion by shaping how users understand and interact with systems. Inclusive microcopy must prioritise clarity, consistency, and empathy.

Key techniques include:

UX Writers should test their work with assistive technology users and in scenarios involving cognitive fatigue or stress. Effective inclusive microcopy reduces support costs, improves task completion rates, and strengthens trust in the product.

# Multimodal UX Design — Inclusive Design Reference

Product-agnostic guidance for designing inclusive, multimodal experiences across mobile, web, large screens, and voice interfaces. Focused on human experience rather than technology or compliance.

**Core principle:** Meaning must survive across senses and interaction methods. The goal is equivalent experiences — equally usable, understandable, and enjoyable — not identical ones.

---

## Philosophy and Foundations

**Human variability is the design baseline.** There is no average user. Ability changes constantly due to sensory differences, physical capability, cognitive processing, emotional state, fatigue, stress, environment, and situational constraints. Inclusive design assumes this variability is normal.

**Modality translation is the mechanism.** Accessibility means translating meaning across sensory channels:

| Original Modality | Equivalent Modalities |
|---|---|
| Sound | Captions, visual indicators, vibration |
| Visual text | Speech output, tactile displays |
| Images | Spoken description, tactile diagrams |
| Touch interaction | Voice commands, keyboard input |
| Audio alerts | Visual cues, haptic signals |

**Equivalent enjoyment, not just equivalent access.** Both experiences can be accessible but feel very different emotionally. Ask: Does each modality feel responsive? Communicate confidence? Support flow rather than interruption?

---

## Input Modalities

Interfaces must support at minimum: touch, keyboard or switch input, voice, pointing devices, and assistive input systems. No single method should be required to complete any action.

**Challenges:** gesture dependency, precision requirements, time-limited actions, pointer-only navigation, keyboard traps.
**Approaches:** provide alternative controls for every interaction; ensure logical navigation order; allow interruption and correction; avoid reliance on speed.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure all tasks can be completed using touch, keyboard-only navigation, voice commands, and pointer input without loss of functionality."

---

## Output Modalities

Systems communicate through visual (text, graphics, motion), audio (speech, sound cues, alerts), and tactile (vibration, force, texture) channels. Important information must never exist in only one modality.

**Challenges:** visual-only information, audio-only alerts, motion without alternatives, colour-only meaning.
**Approaches:** reinforce meaning across modalities; provide equivalents not duplicates; maintain timing synchronisation.
_Prompt:_ "Provide equivalent perceptual access to all information across visual, audio, and tactile modalities so users are not dependent on a single sense."

---

## Design Principles by Topic

**Actionable elements and affordances**
Users must recognise what is interactive. Use clear visual distinction, familiar patterns, predictable outcomes, meaningful labels, and immediate feedback. Avoid ambiguous controls, hidden interactions, and gesture-only features.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure interactive elements are clearly identifiable, labelled, and usable across multiple input methods including assistive technologies."

**Adjustability and personalisation**
Users should control text size, contrast, colour, audio levels, speed and timing, and interaction complexity. Avoid fixed presentation and forced timing. Persist user preferences.
_Prompt:_ "Include adjustable settings allowing users to modify sensory, cognitive, and motor demands according to their needs."

**Choice and multiple pathways**
Provide multiple navigation routes and flexible completion methods. Avoid single workflows or rigid interaction paths.
_Prompt:_ "Provide multiple pathways so users can complete tasks using methods suited to their abilities and preferences."

**Cognitive accessibility**
Design for comprehension and clarity. Use simple language, predictable flows, reduced complexity, and progressive disclosure. Reduce memory burden.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure the experience is understandable for users with varied cognitive abilities and attention levels."

**Consistency and predictability**
Consistent layouts, terminology, and behaviour allow users to build mental models and transfer knowledge. Maintain alignment between visual appearance and functional behaviour.
_Prompt:_ "Specify that patterns must remain consistent across screens and states, with predictable navigation logic and terminology reuse."

**Content structure and hierarchy**
Clear hierarchy improves accessibility across modalities. Define content priority first; maintain logical reading order; use clear grouping; separate meaning from layout.
_Prompt:_ "Structure content so hierarchy is clear without relying on visual layout alone and remains understandable when read aloud by assistive technology."

**Contextual variability**
Users interact in varied environments: noise, lighting, movement, distraction. Designs must adapt by offering alternative modalities, changing feedback intensity, simplifying interaction, and allowing user control. The user should never be trapped in one modality.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure usability across varied environmental conditions without reliance on a single modality."

**Discoverability of access features**
Accessibility features must be easy to find: clear labels, consistent placement, persistent preferences. Avoid hidden settings and complex menus.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure accessibility features are easy to discover, clearly labelled, and consistently located."

**Emotional and sensory experience**
Sound, motion, and haptics shape emotional tone. Modalities influence emotional perception — sound creates urgency, motion creates delight, vibration creates reassurance, voice tone creates trust. Maintain consistent emotional cues across channels.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure emotional tone is consistent across visual, audio, and tactile feedback and supports user confidence."

**Errors, feedback, and recovery**
Errors must never trap users or assign blame. Provide clear next steps, supportive language, and flexible recovery options. All system states (idle, listening, processing, success, error, warning, transition) need consistent signals across modalities.
_Prompt:_ "Design error handling that guides users forward, avoids blame, and provides clear recovery options across modalities."

**Focus management and keyboard access**
All interactive elements must be reachable and operable without pointer input. Design logical navigation sequences following reading order. Provide visible focus indicators. Avoid unexpected focus movement.
_Prompt:_ "Request clear navigation order descriptions, visible focus cues, keyboard-operable interactions, and consistent behaviour when new content appears."

**Form design**
Use explicit labels, contextual hints, and clear error handling. Align input expectations with user mental models. Instructions must remain visible. Errors must be understandable and actionable.
_Prompt:_ "Every input requires a clear descriptive label; error feedback must be understandable and actionable; interaction must work with assistive technologies."

**Iconography**
Icons support comprehension but cannot replace text. Pair icons with text labels. Use established symbols. Design for scalability and contrast. Treat icons as supportive cues, not primary communication tools.
_Prompt:_ "Always include text alternatives alongside icons and avoid relying solely on symbolism for communication."

**Images and non-text content**
Describe purpose and meaning, not appearance. Be concise and contextually relevant. Decorative content must be hidden from assistive technologies. Avoid redundant descriptions.
_Prompt:_ "Provide concise descriptions that communicate the essential meaning, context, and purpose of visual content for users who cannot see it."

**Interaction traps**
Users must always be able to exit any component or state using their chosen input method. Design every interactive element with an obvious, accessible exit pathway. Avoid behaviours that override user navigation unexpectedly.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure users can exit any interaction state easily; provide visible and descriptive escape controls; maintain navigation continuity."

**Media control and autoplay**
Audio must not play automatically. Design around user initiation. Provide clear controls for play, pause, mute, and navigation. Sensory load (motion, sound, visual complexity) should be adjustable.
_Prompt:_ "Avoid automatic playback unless user consent is included; provide clear labelled controls; include captions, transcripts, and visual alternatives."

**Media equivalence**
Media must provide alternatives across modalities: text equivalents for audio, audio equivalents for visuals, synchronised alternatives. Missing synchronisation and audio-only storytelling are common failures.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure all media content includes equivalent alternatives across sensory modalities with synchronised timing."

**Multimodal feedback**
Reinforce important actions with at least two sensory channels. Layer feedback across senses and maintain consistency. Provide state awareness continuously.
_Prompt:_ "Provide multimodal feedback for all important system states, combining at least two sensory channels to reinforce meaning and confidence."

**Responsive and adaptive thinking**
Design for people, not devices. Start with a simple core experience; enhance progressively; maintain consistency across contexts; allow flexible interaction paths.
_Prompt:_ "Create a flexible experience that works across screen sizes, input methods, and capabilities, starting with a simple core interaction and enhancing where possible."

## Platform-Specific Guidance

**Mobile** — Provide voice alternatives for complex typing; use haptic confirmation; ensure screen reader compatibility; provide clear visual hierarchy. _Key question: Can this task be completed without looking at the screen?_

**Web** — Structured semantic content; clear focus states; alternative text for images; captions and transcripts for media. _Key question: If visual design disappeared, would the experience still make sense?_

**Television / Large screens** — High contrast visuals; large readable text; voice input for search; simple navigation depth. _Key question: Can someone understand this from across the room?_

**Voice interfaces** — Confirm actions clearly; provide repeat options; allow interruption; offer multimodal fallback. _Key question: If the system misunderstood, how easily could the user recover?_

---

## Designing for Children and Cognitive Diversity

Reduce text complexity; use visual cues and audio support; design forgiving interactions; provide immediate clarity of goals; support short attention spans and motor skill variability.
_Prompt:_ "Design the experience to be understandable for users with varying cognitive abilities using simple language, visual reinforcement, audio cues, and forgiving interactions."

---

## Touch and Gesture Design

Use large activation areas; provide adequate spacing between targets; offer simple alternatives to complex gestures; avoid hover reliance and accidental activation. Design targets to accommodate limited precision.
_Prompt:_ "Ensure touch interactions are forgiving, have large activation areas, avoid reliance on hover, and provide simple alternatives to complex gestures."

---

## Voice and Conversational Design

Voice removes visual context and increases memory load. Provide balanced choices; confirm actions clearly; allow interruption; avoid repetitive error loops; use supportive tone.
_Prompt:_ "Design conversational interactions that use simple language, confirm actions clearly, allow recovery from misunderstandings, and minimise memory load."

---

## AI as Design Partner — Prompting Principles

AI tools may reproduce inaccessible patterns if not guided explicitly. When prompting for accessible design outcomes:

1. State that accessibility is a core requirement, not an enhancement.
2. Specify multiple input and output modalities.
3. Require compatibility with assistive technologies.
4. Ask for explanations of interaction logic.
5. Request alternatives for sensory or motor differences.
6. Emphasise user control and predictability.
7. Avoid assumptions about user ability.

---

## Multimodal Design Process

1. Identify meaning
2. Map sensory equivalents
3. Map interaction methods
4. Design feedback loops
5. Evaluate context variability
6. Test across modalities and input methods
7. Refine for parity of experience

---

## Testing Approach

Test with: different sensory abilities; different input methods (touch, keyboard, voice, assistive); different environmental contexts (noise, low light, motion); different cognitive loads. Include diverse participants. Do not test only in ideal conditions.
_Prompt:_ "Evaluate the design across multiple sensory conditions, interaction methods, and environments to ensure inclusive usability."

---

## Multimodal Maturity Model

**Level 1 — Single-Mode Awareness:** Primarily visual and pointer-based. Accessibility considered late.
**Level 2 — Alternative Modality Support:** Alternatives provided but treated as secondary experiences.
**Level 3 — Equivalent Multimodal Experiences:** Multimodality intentionally designed for equivalent outcomes from the start.
**Level 4 — Integrated Multimodal Systems:** Multimodal thinking systemic across platforms and teams.
**Level 5 — Adaptive Inclusive Ecosystem:** Experiences dynamically adapt to user context, ability, and environment. Inclusion embedded culturally and strategically.

**Maturity dimensions:** Sensory flexibility · Input flexibility · Platform consistency · Experience parity · Context awareness · Design process integration · Organisational capability.

**Assessment questions for leaders:**
- Do we define inclusion as experience quality or compliance?
- Do designers consider multiple senses during ideation?
- Are flows tested without visual reliance?
- Does our design system include sensory behaviour patterns?
- Do teams expect human variability as a norm?

---

## Key Inclusive Design Questions

**Sensory** — If someone cannot see this, what happens? If someone cannot hear this, what happens?
**Control** — Can this be done without a pointer? Can this be done without speech?
**Context** — What if the user is distracted? What if the environment is noisy?
**Experience** — Does every modality feel equally responsive? Does every modality create confidence?

---

## Core Principles Summary

1. Do not rely on one sense.
2. Support multiple input methods.
3. Provide equivalent experiences.
4. Design for context variability.
5. Enable recovery from errors.
6. Maintain emotional consistency.
7. Prioritise clarity and predictability.
8. Treat accessibility as design quality, not compliance.
